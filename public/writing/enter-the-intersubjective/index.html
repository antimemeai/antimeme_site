Enter the Intersubjective: Wilfrid Sellars as Operations Theorist

Tactical Epistemology?

I had the good fortune (affliction?) to spend a couple years as a philosophy major. Aside from the propensity for writing essays, my takeaway from that period of life was learning to both structure and question things that are easy to take for granted (or even name in some cases, Phenomenology of Spirit, anyone?).

One of these, "how do we know what we know"--epistemology--is central to the drama of navigating unknown unknowns generally, and our present paradigm shift especially. Or so I believe. I have a couple goals for our time together here:

  • Attempt to convince you that using arcane philosophical tools in fast-paced, high-stakes corporate, state, or movement challenges has immediate practical value.

  • Offer a minimally abusive sketch of Sellars' concept of intersubjective knowledge as a powerful tool you can start using today.

  • Tie all of this together with the larger project and current events.

Millenia-old unresolved abstract flamewars can help me how?

If you're bored and feeling a little masochistic, go read a couple theories of knowledge. Someone will make a nice argument, bits of it will resonate with you, and then someone else will come along and shred it--leaving you wondering how the ideas seemed solid to you in the first place.

(Does the Chinese Room know Chinese?)

I'm not here to sell you on trying to solve epistemology. Or on doing epistemology in the ancient flamewar sense. What matters is explicating some view on what it takes to seat and unseat your beliefs to yourself and others (and being open to judicious adjustment).

Consider three common dysfunctions in groups revolving around a divergence in understanding the world:

  • Someone has data but the group argues so extensively on what it is about that the actionable question of what it means is never discussed.

  • Someone who doesn't have data but is seeking feedback on a thoughtful and informed idea is instead told to go get data leading to the previous issue.

  • The group makes decisions based on some graphs but can't agree on an explanation for why this or that graph is actionable in the first place.

One way to evolve past the bulk of these is to define an explicit bar for how the group accepts or rejects beliefs, similar in spirit to the concept of group tenets taught at Amazon. This doesn't mean contriving and then dogmatically worshipping some blind, rigid standard. Instead the idea is to start from established common ground rather than flailing in assumptions.

Revisiting the common dysfunctions in order, tactical epistemology might:

  • Require of an operations or business metric a definition, error statement, and explication of where, how, and when the metric relates to the bigger picture.

  • Safeguard against quantitative traps by constructing a niche for intuition on the basis that data from a system never perfectly describes the system.

  • Make disconfirmation of belief a periodic and supported activity for the group to combat complacency, group-think, and inertia.

Pretty down to earth stuff. Taking a stance on knowledge and belief lies on the same level of abstraction as group tenets, vision, and charter. Like these, writing something down or having a meeting about it is only a start, it takes time to get real value out.

Escaping the Shapeless & the Petrified

Let's get back on the road toward Seeing in a Strange Wilderness. Taking an intersubjective stance on knowledge is a pillar of the project. Great, what is intersubjectivity?

Start from a familiar distinction: subjective vs. objective. One way to define subjective is what things seem like to a particular observer. And objective as the way things actually are incontrovertibly.

Applied to thinking about knowledge and beliefs, subjective knowledge is something like our observer translating what things seem like to her into a self-contained explanation of the world. Objective knowledge then being some flavor of absolute truth about reality that all observers will arrive at given opportunity.

Although way beyond our scope here, there are substantial problems with both taken naively. As much as I'm tempted, we'll take that as Given and drive on.

Intersubjective is a middle path, adding structure to the subjective while dodging the deep and thorny problems plaguing the objective. The idea is that you and I and everyone else around aggregate how things seem to us individually as a group in order to provide checks and balances against flaws in a single perspective. We do this by talking about things until we arrive at a working consensus.

Such aggregation ought to do better than a single random subjective take more often than not, but it's far from sufficient by itself. For one, people collectively agree on things that just aren't true all the time. The saving grace is that the conversation aggregating our subjective perspectives doesn't stop.

What we know intersubjectively today as 13 inches becomes 12.5 inches tomorrow when someone invents a reliable way to mark half-inches. Which some time in the future might become 12.3147326900421 inches using advanced measuring equipment. Regardless, whatever the highest fidelity value for the length in question is right now, based on our collective ability to measure it, is the empirically adequate value for our knowledge of the length.

The key property of empirically adequate beliefs is that they are anchored to a point in time and are subject to refinement or dismissal in the future when capability, information, whatever gets better. They do for now.

[N.B. Of course we don't go around verifying that everyone agrees that grass is green and water is wet all the time. We get a ton of that for free. We do litigate these things when the common path breaks down though. What the hell even is chartreuse? My point here is to illustrate the mechanism. P.]

Alright, I'm Intersubjective! Yeah! Sooo..?

Recall that the whole point of all of these essays is to explicate an approach to dealing with extremely difficult problem spaces inundated with unknown unknowns, some blend of real/perceived risk of ruin, and no real way to avoid it for one reason or another. These properties highlight the charms of an intersubjective stance on knowledge. Specifically:

  • Attack first move paralysis. Taking a best guess first position when the empirically adequate just isn't adequate yet is naturally accounted for.

  • Provide initial structure for the beachhead. Assessing, disconfirming, and improving knowledge become first class operational concerns, form part of your narrative core, and provide necessary (but not sufficient) scaffolding for imposing metrics on the unknown.

  • Bridging connectors for free out of the box. As you advance the plot in mapping your problem space blind, your level of empirical adequacy will improve. Eventually a way to bridge even primitive adequacy in the fraught space with other domains you have mastery in will emerge. An intersubjective stance affords seamless integration of disjoint knowledge trees in such moments.

  • Uplifts the realization of being wrong to a victory*. Especially important as a tool for leaders, the intersubjective stance means that realizing that you're wrong means that you're doing something right. There's neither any indictment of personal judgment as with the subjective nor a shattering of worldview as with the objective.

More on this in future essays, for now though the connection between our ultimate goal and this foray into the arcane is (hopefully) sketched enough to be motivating.

* Assuming the you haven't lost the whole game in one moment--ruin is real but mercifully rare. Staying alive to play again is an accomplishment worth celebrating.

A Word from Our Sponsors: Panic!

Just kidding, although the impulse is seductive. Upheaval, Polycrisis, whatever you want to call it--the constituent elements of the accelerating global change we're enduring today more or less universally challenge beliefs and approaches to knowing that one could get away with taking for granted across a lifetime just a few years ago.

Zooming out from my little essay project here to talk for a moment on a broader human-to-human level, I think taking the time to start developing a habit of working out explicitly how you gain and lose beliefs will benefit you. Whatever your concern, be it AI, the end of Pax Americana, deglobalization, social change, or, most importantly, figuring out how to provide for those you love amidst everything else, the way you see and process the world is under attack right now.

There are many ways to think about how you know something and what that knowledge means. Whether it's an intersubjective stance or something else entirely, now is the time to get serious about accounting for your knowledge and your beliefs.

Outro

In 1940, mathematician G. H. Hardy wrote a moving essay, A Mathematician's Apology, in which he sought to justify having spent his life in the pursuit of pure mathematics for its own sake. Hardy was a committed pacifist writing at the onset of World War II, and he knew what was coming. He wrote passionately that his beloved subfield of math, number theory, was one last bastion of mathematics that could not be applied to war.

His writing beautifully expresses the passions and fears of a life spent on a labor of love, it's worth reading. Professor Hardy, however, could not have been more wrong. Number theory is the basis of cryptography, which is the logical substrate upon which wars of all kinds rage constantly around you. Losing a battle with number theory has devastating real world consequences--ruined businesses, shattered lives, even death.

In like manner, I believe that the dawn of generative AI is epistemology's call to war. Putting daydreams of AGI aside, we've already arrived at the point where planetary-scale spear phishing with high production values is accessible to people and organizations of relatively modest means. Forging a habit of intentionally approaching knowledge and belief into a core self-defense discipline is fast becoming table stakes for life in the 21st century.
